# PE board packs lead with cash, then decompose

A private-equity portfolio-company board pack is not a venture deck with a leverage page bolted on — it is an inverted document. Where a VC deck opens with a CEO narrative and treats cash as a late "runway" slide, the PE sponsor pack **"should lead with cash and variance, then address revenue and EBITDA"** ([Growth Shuttle](https://growthshuttle.com/board-reporting-for-portfolio-companies-why-the-sponsor-pack-and-the-operators-dashboard-disagree/)), carries a covenant-headroom and debt-service page that has no venture analogue, and runs a standing value-creation-plan tracker while cutting the product roadmap, customer stories and org charts a VC deck is built around. The questions that follow that pack are equally distinctive: when a metric deteriorates, sophisticated directors do not ask for an explanation, they ask for a decomposition — a reconciled ARR waterfall, a stage-conversion table, a numerator-versus-denominator split on burn, a distance-to-breach in weeks. The best-sourced material in this whole domain is the credit side, where named thresholds exist (escalate at 15–20% covenant headroom, war-room below 10%, stop initiatives if no improvement by Day 45 — [A Faster Exit](https://afasterexit.com/guides/covenant-headroom/cfo-protocol/)); the most commonly repeated operating triggers, by contrast — NRR below 100%, runway below 12 months, CAC payback above 24 months — **could not be sourced to any citable publication** and should be presented in any product as convention, not as practice. Benchmark publishers now disagree materially enough that averaging them is a mistake: Benchmarkit puts CAC payback at 11 months while High Alpha's $5–50M bands sit at 14–20 ([Benchmarkit 2026](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2026-saas-ai-native-metrics); [High Alpha 2025](https://2994607.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/2994607/2025%20SaaS%20Benchmarks%20Report.pdf)). And across large portfolios, standardization reaches the template but almost never the definition — which is why the single most valuable line a generated board commentary can carry is a footnote naming how each metric was computed.

## PE packs open with cash and covenants, inverting the VC deck

No published, named PE-firm board template exists in public — no Vista, Thoma Bravo, Insight, ICONIQ or K1 pack, and no redacted real portfolio-company deck was located anywhere in this research. That absence is itself a finding: PE sponsors impose a bespoke reporting package defined at close, usually through information covenants in the credit and shareholders agreements, rather than adopting anything downloadable. What can be sourced is the *contents*, and they are consistent across the PE-oriented advisory literature. The required monthly pack carries actual-versus-plan variance with written commentary on each delta; cash position, covenant headroom and debt service coverage ratios; one consistent revenue-recognition definition across every view in the document; pipeline coverage for the next two quarters rather than historical actuals alone; and progress on integration milestones tied to operational initiatives ([Growth Shuttle](https://growthshuttle.com/board-reporting-for-portfolio-companies-why-the-sponsor-pack-and-the-operators-dashboard-disagree/)). A parallel list adds full financial statements against plan, margin and EBITDA bridge analysis, a cash and leverage summary with covenant compliance, a working-capital snapshot, and initiative tracking tied to the value creation plan ([Allegro Grey](https://allegrogrey.com/blog/private-equity-cfo-reporting-kpi-cadence-weekly-flash-and-what-goes-to-the-board/)). Equally instructive is the explicit cut list: **"Activity summaries, feature releases, customer case studies, org charts, and any metric that does not tie to value creation or downside risk"** ([Growth Shuttle](https://growthshuttle.com/board-reporting-for-portfolio-companies-why-the-sponsor-pack-and-the-operators-dashboard-disagree/)). Sponsors read in a stable order — revenue growth against plan, cost control against budget, adjusted EBITDA trending — and Consero's 2026 Investor-backed CFO Report has them prioritising four pillars near-equally: revenue growth 51%, cash flow optimization 51%, EBITDA and margin expansion 50%, digital transformation 50% ([Consero Global](https://conseroglobal.com/resources/board-reporting/)).

Against that, the VC convention is a useful contrast rather than a competitor. Bessemer prescribes the first two slides as a current-priorities page with a **red/yellow/green grading system** against each priority's metric, followed by a forward-priorities page ([Bessemer CFO Playbook](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/cfo-playbook-mastering-metrics-and-managing-boards-for-saas-finance-success)); Mostly Metrics runs a CEO splash slide in 3x3 metric format, then a two-column "what's working / what's not working" page, then P&L and cash burn over **trailing nine quarters** ([Mostly Metrics](https://www.mostlymetrics.com/p/your-complete-guide-to-board-meetings-d92)); Visible.vc's six-section SaaS order puts department updates and the product roadmap ahead of KPIs and financials ([Visible.vc](https://visible.vc/blog/saas-board-deck/)). Merging the sourced PE contents with the sourced VC ordering yields a defensible composite section order — executive summary with RYG scorecard, KPI dashboard versus plan, financials with EBITDA bridge, cash and covenant headroom, bookings and forward pipeline coverage, value-creation-plan tracker, people, a short product section tied to retention, risks, asks, appendix — but that ordering is **this report's synthesis, not a sourced template**. The two pages that reliably distinguish a PE pack from a VC pack are covenant headroom and the VCP tracker; both appear in every PE-oriented source and none of the VC ones.

Cadence matters as much as contents, and the sourced rhythm is more frequent than "quarterly." PE boards convene **approximately 6–10 times per year, most commonly every 6–8 weeks**, with monthly schedules in turnaround or high-growth phases and quarterly treated as "the bare minimum"; materials circulate 3–7 days ahead, directors are expected to spend a minimum of two hours reading, and meetings run 2–4 hours ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/private-equity-board-member-handbook/standard-board-meeting-cadence/)). Beneath the board meeting sit a weekly flash of **one to three pages** covering revenue and bookings versus plan, gross margin direction, EBITDA drivers, cash position and near-term forecast, A/R and A/P signals, leading operational indicators, and key risks with actions underway ([Allegro Grey](https://allegrogrey.com/blog/private-equity-cfo-reporting-kpi-cadence-weekly-flash-and-what-goes-to-the-board/)), and a monthly package that "requires seven to ten business days for honest closure" ([Growth Shuttle](https://growthshuttle.com/board-reporting-for-portfolio-companies-why-the-sponsor-pack-and-the-operators-dashboard-disagree/)). Stack the close against the pre-read window and a monthly pack lands roughly twelve to seventeen business days after month end — which is exactly why sponsors lean on the flash for cash and covenant headroom and why no serious miss should ever debut in a board meeting. No source gives a page-count norm for the pack itself; combining "avoid 100+ slide decks" and a 1.5–2.5 hour meeting ([OnlyCFO](https://www.onlycfo.io/p/board-meetings-that-dont-suck)) with two hours of director prep implies something in the range of twenty to forty core pre-read pages plus an unbounded appendix, but that is inference and should be labelled as such in any tool that sizes output.

## The KPI set is a definition fight before it is a metric list

The shape of every SaaS formula is agreed; the details that change the number are not, and in a PE pack the definition has to be pinned in a footnote because the same label produces materially different values. Start with the ARR bridge, which the board-standard version splits four ways — new, expansion, contraction, churned ARR ([Lets-Viz](https://lets-viz.com/blogs/saas-metrics-for-board-reporting-cfo-s-2026-checklist)) — with the identity Net New ARR = Ending ARR − Beginning ARR = New + Expansion − Contraction − Churn ([The SaaS CFO](https://www.thesaascfo.com/how-to-calculate-the-burn-multiple/)). At least one widely used glossary folds downgrades into "churned ARR" instead of separating contraction ([Driven Insights](https://www.driveninsights.com/small-business-finance-blog/saas-metrics-glossary-align-your-team-on-common-definitions)), and a pack built that way will show worse GRR and overstated logo churn relative to dollar churn. Retention has the same problem one level up: NRR is "the sum of all second-period revenue from customers in the first period who are still customers in the second period, divided by the total first-period revenue," and GRR is the identical calculation **capping each customer's second-period revenue at their first-period level** ([SaaS Capital](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/grrumbling-about-retention-metrics-or-pitfalls-in-measuring-saas-churn/)). That per-customer cap is not the same as the common shortcut of subtracting aggregate expansion from NRR; whenever expansion and contraction coexist in the base, the shortcut flatters GRR.

| Metric | Sourced definition | The variant that changes the answer |
|---|---|---|
| Ending ARR | Total ARR across paying customers with a start date on or before period end and an end date on or after the last day of the period ([Driven Insights](https://www.driveninsights.com/small-business-finance-blog/saas-metrics-glossary-align-your-team-on-common-definitions)) | Whether committed/contracted but unbilled ARR is included — unsourced, varies by company |
| NRR | Second-period revenue from first-period customers ÷ total first-period revenue ([SaaS Capital](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/grrumbling-about-retention-metrics-or-pitfalls-in-measuring-saas-churn/)) | Monthly-vs-year-ago-month series vs a single annual aggregate vs compounded monthly |
| GRR | Same as NRR with each customer capped at first-period revenue ([SaaS Capital](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/grrumbling-about-retention-metrics-or-pitfalls-in-measuring-saas-churn/)) | Per-customer cap vs "NRR minus expansion"; the latter is systematically higher |
| CAC payback | (Average CAC ÷ (Average New ARR × GAAP gross margin)) × 12, in months ([Driven Insights](https://www.driveninsights.com/small-business-finance-blog/saas-metrics-glossary-align-your-team-on-common-definitions)) | Gross-margin-adjusted vs revenue-based; new-customer vs blended |
| CAC ratio | New Customer CAC Ratio = S&M ÷ New Customer ARR; Blended = S&M ÷ (New + Expansion ARR) ([Benchmarkit](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2025benchmarks)) | Which ARR sits in the denominator |
| Magic number | (Current-quarter ARR − prior-quarter ARR) × 4 ÷ prior-quarter S&M ([The SaaS CFO](https://www.thesaascfo.com/calculate-saas-magic-number/)) | Three independent axes: GAAP revenue vs ARR, net vs new ARR, prior vs current quarter S&M ([Driven Insights](https://www.driveninsights.com/small-business-finance-blog/saas-metrics-glossary-align-your-team-on-common-definitions)) |
| Burn multiple | Net burn ÷ net new ARR, net burn taken from the cash flow statement ([The SaaS CFO](https://www.thesaascfo.com/how-to-calculate-the-burn-multiple/)) | Cash-flow burn (captures capitalized software, capex, working capital) vs EBITDA proxy |
| Runway | Ending cash ÷ average cash burned over the last 3 months, operating burn excluding financing ([Driven Insights](https://www.driveninsights.com/small-business-finance-blog/saas-metrics-glossary-align-your-team-on-common-definitions)) | Trailing-3-month vs forecast burn; with vs without undrawn revolver |
| Rule of 40 | Revenue growth % + profitability margin % ([Mostly Metrics](https://www.mostlymetrics.com/p/the-right-way-to-calculate-rule-of-40)) | EBITDA vs FCF vs adjusted EBITDA — Snowflake scores 43% on adjusted EBITDA and 0% on EBITDA |
| Leverage ratio | Net debt ÷ EBITDA, LTM, tested rolling quarterly ([Nilus](https://www.nilus.com/blog/covenant-compliance-monitoring-the-pe-backed-cfos-complete-playbook/)) | Lender-defined EBITDA, not accounting EBITDA — the most common compliance error |

The magic number deserves special attention in any automated tool because its three variants are independent, meaning a pack that reports "magic number" without specifying revenue-vs-ARR, net-vs-new and prior-vs-current-quarter S&M is not comparable across quarters if the FP&A owner changes. Burn has the analogous trap in reverse: because net burn per The SaaS CFO includes capitalized software development, a company that capitalizes engineering shows a better EBITDA *and* a worse burn multiple than one that expenses it, which is precisely why PE packs carry both. The Rule of 40 disagreement is the most consequential of all for a sub-$50M company, and the source that measured it across 148 public tech companies is candid about the limits of its own recommendation: **"Adjusted EBITDA is not cleaner or truer. It is simply the only thing that does not blow up when you try to rank 148 companies with completely different financial plumbing"** ([Mostly Metrics, 7 Dec 2025](https://www.mostlymetrics.com/p/the-right-way-to-calculate-rule-of-40)).

Then there is the PE-only layer, which is where the KPI page genuinely diverges from anything a venture board sees. Leverage below 4.0x–5.0x and interest coverage above 2.0x–3.0x are typical covenant thresholds; fixed charge coverage, tested as (EBITDA − CapEx − Taxes) ÷ fixed charges, is more restrictive and its definitions "rarely align with leverage covenant definitions"; minimum-liquidity covenants are often tested on a spot basis, so **a single adverse day can breach** ([Nilus](https://www.nilus.com/blog/covenant-compliance-monitoring-the-pe-backed-cfos-complete-playbook/)). Covenant headroom is best understood not as a finance metric but as a governance one — "the margin between a company's current financial performance and the point where lenders gain leverage over decisions, measured as distance-to-breach across leverage, coverage, and liquidity covenants" ([A Faster Exit](https://afasterexit.com/guides/covenant-headroom/)). A practical consequence: a levered PE portfolio company effectively runs two EBITDA columns, management's and the lender's, and an unlevered venture company runs none. For the KPI page itself, the only sourced core set is a five-metric recommendation — ARR, NRR, Rule of 40, CAC payback, burn multiple — with the ARR waterfall and Rule-of-40 components shown as trended visuals and CAC payback and burn multiple segmented by acquisition channel ([Lets-Viz](https://lets-viz.com/blogs/saas-metrics-for-board-reporting-cfo-s-2026-checklist)). **The column convention — actual, prior period, budget, variance, full-year plan — is unsourced.** No retrievable source specifies it for a PE SaaS board page, and any tool that hard-codes one should say so.

One methodological point belongs in a product spec. SaaS Capital's recommended retention method is explicitly neither annualized monthly churn nor a single annual figure: **compare each month to the same month a year earlier, then track 3-, 6- and 12-month averages of that series**. Month-over-month retention "is extremely sensitive to which two months you use," and annual aggregation conceals quarters — their worked example shows a 12-month aggregate GRR of 87.5% hiding a Q4 in which GRR fell from 100% to 50% ([SaaS Capital](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/grrumbling-about-retention-metrics-or-pitfalls-in-measuring-saas-churn/)). The implication for a board pack is to plot the monthly-versus-year-ago series with 3-month and 12-month moving averages together, using the short average as the early warning and the long one as the valuation-relevant line. A trailing-twelve-month NRR will still read above 100% for some months after in-period NRR has broken below it; no source quantifies that lag, so do not assert a number for it.

## Benchmark publishers disagree by nine months on CAC payback

The most granular, most recent and most ARR-band-relevant source for small-cap software is High Alpha's 2025 SaaS Benchmarks Report, the successor to OpenView's series, with 800+ respondents and median/upper/lower quartiles cut by ARR band ([High Alpha 2025](https://2994607.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/2994607/2025%20SaaS%20Benchmarks%20Report.pdf)). Its shape tells the whole small-cap story: growth decays steeply with scale while efficiency improves, and retention medians stay nearly flat — it is dispersion, not the median, that narrows.

| Metric ($5–20M ARR / $20–50M ARR, median · UQ · LQ) | $5–20M | $20–50M |
|---|---|---|
| NRR | 103% · 115% · 95% | 103% · 110% · 98% |
| GRR | 88% · 95% · 82% | 90% · 95% · 85% |
| YoY ARR growth | 31% · 72% · 15% | 30% · 41% · 16% |
| Rule of 40 | 20 · 35 · −3 | 24 · 41 · 11 |
| CAC payback (months; UQ is the *longer*, worse tail) | 14 · 22 · 8 | 20 · 27 · 11 |
| Software gross margin | 80% · 86% · 70% | 78% · 84% · 71% |
| S&M as % of ARR | 30% · 53% · 22% | 29% · 34% · 24% |
| ARR per employee | $166.7K · $220.6K · $130.4K | $268.2K · $350.0K · $157.4K |

All figures: [High Alpha 2025 SaaS Benchmarks Report](https://2994607.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/2994607/2025%20SaaS%20Benchmarks%20Report.pdf).

Three conflicts between publishers must be shown rather than averaged. **First, CAC payback.** Benchmarkit's 2026 edition reports 11 months ([Benchmarkit 2026](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2026-saas-ai-native-metrics)) against High Alpha's 14 months at $5–20M and 20 at $20–50M. Neither publisher's formula was retrievable from public pages, and the samples differ; the likeliest explanation is a gross-profit-adjusted versus revenue-based denominator combined with a different panel. **Second, the direction of gross retention.** Benchmarkit shows GRR deteriorating from 90% in 2022 to 88% in the FY2024 data to **84% in its 2026 edition**, with the 75th percentile falling from 95% to 91% ([Benchmarkit 2025](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2025benchmarks); [Benchmarkit 2026](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2026-saas-ai-native-metrics)); KeyBanc/Sapphire report GRR falling to 86% in 2023 and **expected to approach 90%** ([KeyBanc 2025](https://investor.key.com/press-releases/news-details/2025/PRIVATE-SAAS-COMPANY-SURVEY-REVEALS-AI-DRIVEN-TRANSFORMATION-AND-SUSTAINED-OPERATIONAL-EXCELLENCE/default.aspx)); SaaS Capital reports retention essentially flat in its bootstrapped $3–20M cohort ([SaaS Capital](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/benchmarking-metrics-for-bootstrapped-saas-companies/)). Treat "GRR is recovering" as contested; note also that Benchmarkit retitled its 2026 edition "SaaS & AI-Native Metrics," signalling a panel composition change that may make the 84% a mix effect rather than like-for-like deterioration, and that much of the KeyBanc headline data is forward-looking expectation, not realised result. **Third, sales and marketing spend.** Benchmarkit puts S&M at 37% of revenue at median, splitting **47% for VC-backed against 33% for PE-backed** ([Benchmarkit 2025](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2025benchmarks)); SaaS Capital's departmental splits imply roughly 23% of ARR ([SaaS Capital spending](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/spending-benchmarks-for-private-b2b-saas-companies/)). That fourteen-point gap is a sample-and-definition artefact — SaaS Capital's panel is bootstrapped-heavy and its department taxonomy differs — and the two should never be presented as a single range.

ICONIQ's 2026 numbers are the most quoted and the most dangerous to use as a comparator. Its "Pacesetters" cohort — an AI-forward cut of an elite, ICONIQ-selected 137-company panel — shows **120% median net dollar retention under $100M ARR and a 0.8x burn multiple at scale**, against **99–102% median NDR for all other software companies** and 1.4x burn ([ICONIQ 2026](https://www.iconiq.com/growth/reports/2026-state-of-scaling)). The 120% is a winners' cut, not a population benchmark. Two further gaps bind any tool built on these numbers: **no publisher located gives a magic number distribution by ARR band for private sub-$100M companies**, and **burn multiple by ARR band under $100M is unpublished** — ICONIQ's figures are $100M+ and High Alpha publishes absolute monthly dollar burn instead. Likewise, SMB/mid-market/enterprise cuts of NRR, GRR, CAC payback and S&M are not published in the free material of any major report; the honest substitute is an ACV cut, and the one sourced anchor is that $10K–$100K ACV shows "the strongest balance of scale and durability" — above 30% growth, GRR near or above 90%, NRR above 104% ([High Alpha 2025](https://2994607.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/2994607/2025%20SaaS%20Benchmarks%20Report.pdf)).

Finally, the framing point that matters most when a generated commentary reaches for a threshold: **do not use Rule of 40 as a pass/fail bar for a sub-$50M company.** Published median is 20–25 and the top quartile at $5–20M ARR is only 35, so a "40" line places a genuinely upper-quartile company in the fail column. On the same logic, a 14–20 month CAC payback at $5–50M is at the median, not a red flag; the concerning zone begins past roughly 22–27 months, which is where the lower quartile sits.

## Deteriorating metrics trigger decomposition demands, not explanations

Very little published material records actual PE board dialogue. What exists comes from governance publishers, growth-equity firms publishing CEO-facing self-diagnostics, credit advisory content, and sales-management literature — and the metric-diagnostic sources describe the *analysis* a question is demanding rather than the words. That distinction should be preserved in any generated output.

**When NRR falls,** the load-bearing question is not "why" but "decompose it." The instruction is verbatim: **"Rebuild the monthly waterfall for the last eight quarters so the components reconcile to ending ARR,"** separating churned customer ARR, churned product ARR, product decrease ARR, new customer ARR, new product ARR and increased product ARR; and **"Isolate the month, isolate the cohort, then act on the component,"** on the claim that most NRR declines trace to specific customer cohorts renewing in particular months rather than to a company-wide trend ([ORM, 15 Jun 2026](https://orm-tech.com/blog/why-net-revenue-retention-dropped)). The sharpest escalation test in this entire domain comes from the same source: **"Falling gross with flat net is the pattern to escalate on, because it means the retention engine is broken and the sales motion is paying for it."** Component-level diagnostics follow — loss reasons and tenure of churned accounts, seat utilisation versus contracted seats in downgraded accounts, attach rate on renewals this year versus last — each stated as an analysis rather than a question. An investor-facing ARR-quality diagnostic supplies genuinely verbatim questions: "What percentage of ARR sits on annual or multi-year agreements versus month-to-month terms?", "What proportion does your largest customer represent? What share comes from your top five and top ten accounts?", "How do churn and downgrades distribute across your customer base? Are losses concentrated in specific segments or cohorts?" and "Where does ARR growth originate — new customers versus expansion?", all on the premise that **"two companies reporting $5 million in ARR can have fundamentally different risk profiles"** ([G-Squared CFO, 9 Feb 2026](https://www.gsquaredcfo.com/blog/arr-quality)). A gap worth flagging: no source records verbatim director questions about renewal *timing* or a named "at-risk ARR" line item, despite both being standard practice.

**When burn runs over budget,** the dominant published frame is the burn multiple, defined as net burn ÷ net new ARR and explicitly positioned as a catch-all: **"Any serious problem will eventually impact the Burn Multiple"** — gross margin, sales efficiency, churn, growth and founder capability all surface in it ([David Sacks, 23 Apr 2020](https://sacks.substack.com/p/the-burn-multiple-51a7e43cb200)). The board question that follows is diagnostic-in-reverse: which of those four actually moved. Because it is a ratio, a director will also want it split — did net burn rise, or did net new ARR fall? — though no source phrases that split as a board question. **This is the most significant sourcing gap in the deterioration material: no published, attributable board question about budget variance on burn or EBITDA could be located**, despite it being universal practice. Any such question in a generated pack is a construction and should be labelled one. Sacks's bands (<1.0x amazing, 1.0–1.5x great, 1.5–2.0x good, 2.0–3.0x suspect, >3.0x bad, as rendered by [The SaaS CFO](https://www.thesaascfo.com/how-to-calculate-the-burn-multiple/)) are venture-stage; no PE-specific burn multiple threshold for control-buyout software assets exists in public.

**When runway falls under 12 months,** the best-sourced questions come from the credit side, which is exactly what a PE director is pre-empting on the sponsor's behalf. Verbatim: **"How much cushion remains after downside sensitivity?"**, **"Which add-backs will the lender haircut?"**, **"What happens if EBITDA misses by 5–10%?"**, and **"When did the lender last receive a forecast — and has it changed since?"** The credit committee's own four questions, which a director will put to management first, are **"Is EBITDA sustainable?"** ("one good quarter doesn't erase six bad ones"), **"Are adjustments reversible?"** ("add-backs get scrutinized under stress"), **"Is there liquidity behind the compliance?"** ("covenant math without cash runway signals danger"), and **"Does the sponsor have our back?"** ("equity support indicates confidence; silence signals exit planning") ([A Faster Exit, Jan 2026](https://afasterexit.com/guides/covenant-headroom/cfo-protocol/)). The same source supplies the only genuinely sourced escalation ladder in this domain: **headroom below 20% triggers weekly exec review and lender narrative prep; below 10% triggers a daily war room with the sponsor engaged; add-backs above 35% of EBITDA should be assumed to attract a 20–40% haircut; no improvement by Day 45 means stop initiatives and redirect resources** — with engagement beginning when headroom drops below 15–20% and lender escalation typically 4–8 weeks before test dates ([A Faster Exit](https://afasterexit.com/guides/covenant-headroom/)). A complementary banded scheme expresses alerts as a percentage of the covenant limit — 80% early awareness, 90% planning window, 95% action required, which on a 4.0x leverage covenant means alerts at 3.2x, 3.6x and 3.8x ([Nilus](https://www.nilus.com/blog/covenant-compliance-monitoring-the-pe-backed-cfos-complete-playbook/)). On hearing bad news, the two questions a board asks are **"What is the worst outcome here?"** and **"How likely is that outcome?"**, and the standard directors hold a CFO to is blunt: **"Directors will lose patience quickly if you can't be precise about knowable data points"** ([CFO Secrets](https://www.cfosecrets.io/p/cfo-role-boardroom)).

**A caution that belongs in the product, not a footnote.** The escalation triggers most commonly repeated in practice — NRR below 100%, runway below 12 months, CAC payback above 24 months — **could not be sourced to any citable publication in this research**. No source was found for a PE escalation trigger at NRR below 100%, none for a runway threshold in a PE portfolio-monitoring context, and none for a CAC payback threshold in months for portfolio companies. The reasoning behind them is sound (NRR at 100% is where the installed base stops self-funding growth, and Benchmarkit's 101% median means a sub-100% company is below median as well) but reasoning is not sourcing. The defensible design move is to borrow the credit-side mechanic instead: express each operating KPI as a percentage of its plan or covenant-equivalent floor and band it at 80/90/95, rather than asserting folk thresholds as practice.

**When pipeline rises but bookings do not,** the published sales literature converges on one move — stop accepting rep narrative and inspect buyer-side evidence. Verbatim questions, each aimed at a deal state: for late-stage opportunities **"Why hasn't this deal closed yet?"**; for stalled deals **"What's the buyer's next step, and when will it happen?"**; for new deals **"What problem is this prospect solving and what is the impact?"** The inspection checks are equally usable: **"Are close dates realistic or repeatedly pushed?"**, "Can reps articulate the buyer's problems clearly?", "Is there a scheduled next step with clear buyer action?", and **"Has the cost of inaction been quantified?"** — the framework's stated principle being to prioritise buyer-driven activities over rep narratives ([A Sales Growth Company, 26 Feb 2025](https://salesgrowth.com/sales-pipeline-review-playbook/)). At board level the register shifts from individual deals to distributions, and the win-rate diagnostic literature is prescriptive: **"Confirm the decline survives segmentation"** by segment, lead source, rep tenure and deal size before concluding anything, and **"Calculate conversion for each stage entered, because late stage losses and early stage losses point to opposite fixes"** ([ORM, 3 Aug 2026](https://orm-tech.com/blog/why-is-my-win-rate-dropping)). The same source directs attention to discount depth on won deals versus last year, "no decision" loss reasons, competitor clustering and territory-specific declines. A growth-equity partner's standing question completes the set: **"How quickly are you improving your sales and implementation cycle time?"** ([Mainsail Partners, 17 Jul 2024](https://mainsailpartners.com/20-questions-every-ceo-should-ask-while-building-a-saas-company/)). Pipeline-up/bookings-flat resolves into one of three sourced sub-findings: stage conversion fell, cycle time lengthened, or the pipeline was never buyer-validated. Notably absent from the public record: verbatim board questions on quota attainment distribution, rep ramp, pipeline coverage ratios, or in-period-created versus aged pipeline.

**On definitions and restatements, the record is essentially empty.** No published example of a board question about a restated SaaS metric or a mid-period definition change could be located. What is sourced is the norm whose violation provokes the question: metrics **must tie out across presenters**, must carry year-over-year and plan comparisons consistently, and must keep identical formatting quarter to quarter, because "the lack of consistency in reporting makes it hard to compare board meeting to board meeting" ([Mostly Metrics](https://www.mostlymetrics.com/p/your-complete-guide-to-board-meetings-d92)). The detection mechanism follows from the trailing-nine-quarter table format: a director who keeps last quarter's pack notices a changed definition as a discontinuity in a *historical* figure, not the current one ([Kellblog](https://www.kellblog.com/the-first-three-slides-of-a-saas-board-deck-with-company-key-metrics/)). Any definitional-integrity question in a generated pack is therefore a construction on a sourced norm.

## Investors interrogate assumptions; operators volunteer numbers

The phrasing asymmetry is the best-sourced qualitative finding here, and it resolves into a simple rule: investors ask questions whose object is an assumption, operators make statements whose object is a number. The PE board handbook publishes explicit before/after pairs that are directly usable as an editing rule — poor: **"Why are we failing to hit our sales targets?"** → better: **"What have we learned about our sales performance this quarter, and how can we adjust our approach?"**; poor: **"This initiative seems off track"** → better: **"What additional resources or changes in approach could help us accelerate progress?"** Its model follow-ups are **"Are these challenges specific to one region or product line, or are they systemic?"** and "What lessons have we learned from delays, and how can we apply these insights to other projects?", and its stated purposes for any board question are to clarify ambiguities, challenge assumptions underlying strategic decisions, and explore risks not fully addressed — framed to "invite thoughtful discussion rather than defensiveness" ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/private-equity-board-member-handbook/formulating-questions-and-discussion-points/)). The transformation rule is mechanical enough to automate: strip the accusation, keep the object, add a forward verb.

The best published test of substance versus theatre is a single question — **"What are we assuming that, if wrong, would materially change this decision?"** Performative governance looks like smooth meetings, polished presentations, polite questions and early adjournment with no testing of underlying assumptions; boards mistake "a healthy exchange of views" for oversight, reflecting "process comfort rather than decision consequence." Substantive governance requires naming a specific assumption, explaining how it was validated, and defining what evidence would indicate failure — **"naming an assumption, not a mood"** — and the diagnostic value lies partly in the answer: when executives respond with defensive vagueness or pivot back to the deck, directors learn something about management's decision discipline ([Aspirations Group, 17 Sep 2026](https://www.aspirations-group.com/post/the-board-question-that-separates-good-governance-from-theater)). Director-education guidance adds craft: **"Before you pose a question, ask yourself, 'What's the purpose?'"**, since purpose determines wording, timing and tone; on contentious issues "framing it as a question is almost always a safe way to do it"; and probing questions "can be tough to ask without triggering defensiveness" and need clear, respectful framing ([DirectorPrep](https://savvy.directorprep.com/blog/choose-your-questions-wisely)).

The operator register is the mirror image. A PE-backed-CEO guide tells the chief executive to "address both positive and negative aspects," treat transparency as key, and in the State of the Union "be open and share what keeps you up at night as a leader" ([New Harbor Capital, 14 May 2024](https://blog.newharborcap.com/en/board-meetings-private-equity-backed-ceo)). A venture partner frames the CEO's job as bringing the board a genuine question — "Is there a key strategic issue you want the board's thinking on?" — with deep dives into things like the product roadmap or sales hiring plan ([SaaStr / Ajay Agarwal, 4 Feb 2022](https://www.saastr.com/the-10-keys-to-a-successful-board-meeting-how-to-make-them-great-and-not-something-everyone-dreads-from-ajay-agarwal-bain-capital-partner/)). The failure modes mirror too: the director who performs rigour without naming an assumption, and the CEO who performs transparency without naming a number. There is a real tension between the two strands worth building into any tone setting — the handbook's non-defensive, learning-framed style can itself shade into the polite-question theatre the governance critique attacks. The resolution is that softness of *tone* is fine provided the *object* of the question is a specific assumption or a specific number. That is the design constraint for generated questions: warm verb, hard object.

One structural observation about the "questions every board should ask" genre, which spans a ten-question governance list ([Corporate Governance Institute, 4 Dec 2025](https://www.thecorporategovernanceinstitute.com/insights/news-analysis/questions-every-board-should-ask/)), a twenty-question strategy list ([CPA Canada, 3rd ed. 2012](https://capacitycanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/20_questions_directors_should_ask_about_strategy.pdf)) and a SaaS-specific twenty ([Mainsail Partners](https://mainsailpartners.com/20-questions-every-ceo-should-ask-while-building-a-saas-company/)): it is almost entirely *ex ante* and structural — agenda, strategy, risk appetite, succession — and almost never *ex post* and diagnostic. **None of the canonical lists contains a question about a missed quarter.** That is why the deterioration questions above come from operating and finance literature rather than governance literature, and it is the clearest signal that a tool generating post-hoc diagnostic questions is filling a real gap rather than restating a known list.

## Ten questions for a company at 97% NRR and 11 months runway

The scenario: net revenue retention has fallen from **108% to 97% over three quarters**; pipeline is rising while bookings are not; burn is **20% over budget**; runway is **11 months**. Benchmark placement first, because a diagnosis paragraph should anchor before it accuses. At $5–20M ARR, 97% NRR sits between the lower quartile (95%) and median (103%); at $20–50M it sits just below the lower quartile of 98% ([High Alpha 2025](https://2994607.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/2994607/2025%20SaaS%20Benchmarks%20Report.pdf)). The eleven-point slide over three quarters, not the level, is the signal — and the company has crossed from "installed base self-funds growth" to "it does not."

**A worked diagnosis paragraph** (the shape a generated commentary should take — anchor, decomposition demand, cross-metric link, and the one thing that must be true):

> NRR has fallen eleven points across three quarters, from 108% to 97%, moving the company from roughly the $5–20M ARR median (103%) to just above the lower quartile (95%) on High Alpha's 2025 bands. Because the decline is sequential rather than a single-quarter step, the working hypothesis is a cohort concentrated in particular renewal months rather than a company-wide trend, and the pack does not yet contain the monthly ARR waterfall needed to confirm or reject it. Two facts sit uncomfortably together: pipeline is rising while bookings are flat, and burn is 20% over budget — which means the numerator of the burn multiple is rising while the denominator, net new ARR, is being eroded from both ends. With 11 months of runway, the deterioration has a date attached to it rather than a trend line. The single most important unanswered question is whether gross retention has fallen alongside net; if it has held while net fell, this is a pricing and expansion problem, and if it has fallen too, the retention engine itself is broken and the sales motion has been paying for it. *[Note: no sourced escalation trigger exists at NRR below 100% or runway below 12 months; these are treated here as salience thresholds, not as standards.]*

**The ten questions**, grouped by theme. Provenance is marked: **[V]** verbatim from the cited source; **[V+]** verbatim question or instruction with the company's values inserted; **[C]** constructed by this report on a sourced finding, quotable to no one.

And when the diagnosis is genuinely ambiguous, the sourced generic follow-up is **"Are these challenges specific to one region or product line, or are they systemic?"** ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/private-equity-board-member-handbook/formulating-questions-and-discussion-points/)).

Three generation rules fall out of the above. First, **every question names a metric and its value** — this is not stylistic, it is what separates the sourced questions from the performative ones; the governance critique's whole point is that a question with a mood as its object is theatre. Second, **prefer the decomposition demand to the causal question**: "rebuild the waterfall so components reconcile" outperforms "why did NRR fall," and a management team that answers a waterfall demand with a narrative has revealed it has not done the work. Third, **soften the verb, never the object**: apply the strip-the-accusation/keep-the-object/add-a-forward-verb rule, so "why are we 20% over budget" becomes "what have we learned about the cost base this quarter, and which of the four burn-multiple drivers moved."

## Standardization stops at the template and never reaches the definition

Across large portfolios, data collection sits on a three-mode spectrum — templates, spreadsheet ingestion, and direct connections to portfolio company applications — with an optional managed data service where the vendor's own team chases and keys the numbers, a tier "valued by some firms, rejected by others" ([PE Stack](https://www.pestack.com/research/product-in-focus-series-gp-portfolio-monitoring)). The existence of that paid tier is strong indirect evidence that chasing and normalizing submissions is labour-intensive enough that firms will pay someone else to absorb it. How automated is any of this? The best independent proxy is EY's CFO-respondent survey: only **27% of firms above $15B AUM describe their platform as "highly automated," falling to 17% at $2.5B–$15B and 14% below $2.5B**, with roughly **74% of mid-size and smaller firms running a combination of manual and automated processes** ([EY 2023 Global Private Equity Survey](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-us/insights/private-equity/documents/ey-2023-private-equity-survey.pdf)). Those figures cover the whole operating platform rather than monitoring specifically, but they bound the ceiling: if fewer than a third of mega-funds are highly automated overall, system-to-system integration with dozens of portfolio companies is a minority practice. The vendor landscape reflects the same immaturity — an independent tracker counts roughly **127 vendors** touching portfolio monitoring of which only **24% treat it as their primary business**, narrowing to about **40 viable** under stricter criteria, with **39% founded since 2016** ([PE Stack](https://www.pestack.com/research/product-in-focus-series-gp-portfolio-monitoring)); consolidation is underway, FactSet having acquired Cobalt Software in October 2021 ([FactSet](https://investor.factset.com/news-releases/news-release-details/factset-acquires-cobalt-software-inc)).

The problem being solved is heterogeneity, not arithmetic. ILPA's own framing when it released the Portfolio Company Metrics Template was that portfolio company information "currently comes in diverse, customized formats that require significant time and effort from both GPs and LPs to create, share, and analyze," and the template's purpose was "to reduce the resources required by General and Limited Partners alike" ([ILPA, 19 Mar 2019](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/PortCo-Reporting-Final-Press-Release-3.19.19.pdf)). It was built by a 30-organization working group and is **voluntary — there is no enforcement mechanism**, and its adoption rate is not published anywhere. A refresh is in flight: public comment closed 2 October 2026 with **final release planned for January 2027**, promising "streamlined, yet detailed deal-level exposure data, company-specific KPIs, and investment transaction details" ([ILPA template hub](https://ilpa.org/industry-guidance/templates-standards-model-documents/ilpa-portfolio-company-metrics-template/)) — a moving target for anyone rebuilding reporting infrastructure now. The critical distinction, often blurred in vendor material: **ILPA standardizes what is reported to LPs about a portfolio company; it does not standardize how that company computes ARR or maps its general ledger.** A firm adopting ILPA still needs its own KPI dictionary and mapping layer. The two artifacts solve different problems.

The failure modes practitioners describe — definitional drift across companies and between a company's finance team and the deal model, silent restatements that invalidate already-issued packs, late submissions that make the pack only as timely as its slowest contributor, chart-of-accounts and CRM-stage mismatch that breaks whenever a company re-segments, and plain spreadsheet error — are **not backed by any independent study**. No published research quantifies submission lateness, restatement frequency or error rates for PE portfolios, and no public data exists on whether monitoring platforms improve accuracy rather than merely centralizing. Buy-and-build software portfolios compound the problem: every add-on brings a new GL, a new billing system and new definitions into a company that had just been standardized. The governance asymmetry underneath all of this is the finding a reporting tool should be designed around: **fund-level NAV and valuations receive an annual audit and often an independent valuation provider, while the portfolio company operating KPIs that drive board decisions and LP narrative generally receive neither** — no assurance, no defined restatement policy and no audit trail unless the firm builds one. The regulatory backstop is absent too: the Fifth Circuit vacated the SEC's Private Fund Adviser Rules in full on 5 June 2024, including the quarterly statement rule, though many LPs had already negotiated equivalent rights into side letters, so obligations persist contractually and heterogeneously across LPs in the same fund ([Morgan Lewis, June 2024](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/06/fifth-circuit-vacates-sec-private-fund-adviser-rules-in-full)). Whether the SEC has since re-proposed a replacement was not verified in this research and should be checked before any product asserts the current position.

On AI, the credible evidence shows a wide pilot base and a thin production base. Bain surveyed firms managing $3.2 trillion in assets in September 2024 and found a **majority of portfolio companies in some phase of generative AI testing but nearly 20% with operationalized use cases producing concrete results** ([Bain, Global Private Equity Report 2025](https://www.bain.com/insights/field-notes-from-generative-ai-insurgency-global-private-equity-report-2025/)); at a McKinsey forum of **65 PE operating partners, about 60% said portfolio companies were experimenting and only about 5% reported production-scale implementations** ([McKinsey, 1 Aug 2024](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/private-capital/our-insights/a-clear-eyed-view-of-gen-ai-for-the-private-equity-industry)). Those are differently thresholded rather than contradictory, and the direction is the same. The striking asymmetry: every quantified AI win in Bain's material sits inside a portfolio company's own P&L — sales response time, customer care, content production, code generation — and **no source quantifies AI savings in the GP's own KPI collection, normalization or board reporting**. The reporting workflow is simultaneously the most obviously automatable thing in portfolio operations and the least evidenced. McKinsey's own framing is the defensible position for a tool in this space: the applicable archetype is the **"virtual knowledge worker" — summarizing unstructured data and extracting insights** — and the operating rule is to treat gen AI "as an intern" whose output requires critical evaluation, keeping humans in the loop against hallucination, bias and compliance exposure. Extraction, normalization suggestions and draft commentary are in scope; unreviewed numbers in a board pack are not.

## Conclusion

The most useful thing this research establishes is a hierarchy of epistemic confidence that a generated board pack should wear on its sleeve. The credit layer is hard: covenant definitions, headroom bands, add-back haircuts and escalation ladders are published, specific and mutually consistent, and they give a tool real thresholds to fire on. The diagnostic layer is medium: the analyses a board demands when a metric slips are well documented, but almost none of them exist as verbatim questions, so anything phrased as a question is an act of rendering that should be labelled. The escalation layer for operating metrics is soft to the point of being folklore — the NRR-below-100, runway-below-12, payback-above-24 triad is repeated everywhere and sourced nowhere. A product that presents all three layers in the same typeface will eventually embarrass its user in front of a director who asks where a number came from. The design answer is to borrow the credit mechanic for the soft layer: band each operating KPI against its own plan or floor at 80/90/95 rather than against a folk threshold, and reserve absolute triggers for the covenant metrics where they are actually published.

The second implication is about what a board pack is for. The PE literature keeps circling the same idea from different directions — the pack is "for governance, evaluation, and risk oversight" while the dashboard is "for daily operating decisions" ([Growth Shuttle](https://growthshuttle.com/board-reporting-for-portfolio-companies-why-the-sponsor-pack-and-the-operators-dashboard-disagree/)); two-thirds of board time should go to decisions not yet made ([Bessemer](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/cfo-playbook-mastering-metrics-and-managing-boards-for-saas-finance-success)); the best question names an assumption, not a mood. The corollary for a tool whose main output is a diagnosis paragraph and ten questions is that the questions are the product and the deck is the substrate. A pack that merely reports 97% NRR has done nothing a spreadsheet could not; a pack that reports 97% NRR, places it between the lower quartile and median of a named benchmark band, states which decomposition would resolve the ambiguity, and hands the board a question whose object is that decomposition has changed what happens in the room. That is also the sharpest commercial observation available here: the governance question-lists are entirely ex ante and the diagnostic literature is entirely unrendered, which leaves the post-miss, metric-anchored board question as a genuinely unoccupied space rather than a restatement of something already published.
